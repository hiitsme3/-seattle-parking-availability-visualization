1. The Shape of Seattle's Parking Network

Seattle’s parking supply is split between curb-side spaces along streets and structured public garages. We start with a plain basemap of the city’s core.

As you scroll, we’ll add layers that show where the city regulates curb space and where garages are available.

2. Paid Area Curb Spaces

In this view, we reveal paid area curb spaces. These are block faces where drivers must pay to park — a signal that demand is high enough that simply leaving parking free would cause problems.

Notice how coverage clusters around Downtown and nearby commercial streets.

3. Where You Actually Can’t Park

But not every curb segment is usable. Many blocks are signed as no parking, no stopping, bus-only, loading-only, or special-purpose zones.

In this view, red curb segments mark places where a typical driver can’t just park and walk away. Blue segments are more realistically available to most drivers.

Toggle to hide red restricted curb segments and see only curb space a typical driver can use.

4. Public Garages

Next we hide the curb segments and highlight public garages. Each point represents a garage or lot where drivers can pay to park off-street.

Garages tend to appear where space is constrained and demand is strong — often aligned with the same commercial cores.

5. Curb vs Garages: Overlap

With both layers visible, we can see parking supply hotspots — places where drivers have both managed curb space and structured garages within walking distance.

7. Hotspots Appear

These glowing points highlight parking supply hotspots: blocks where dense paid curb segments and at least one public garage sit within a short walk.

Main takeaway: These are the rare places where curb convenience and garage capacity stack together. These are the spots where drivers are encouraged to go straight to a nearby garage, instead of driving in circles trying to find curb parking.

8. Zooming into Downtown

Zooming into central Seattle shows how this balance plays out block by block. Each blue line is a paid curb segment on a main street, and each circle is a public garage on an interior lot or side street.

Main takeaway: this zoom explains why parking feels scarce even when capacity exists: drivers notice curb spaces on main streets first and often overlook nearby garages tucked just out of view.

9. What the Data Suggests

Across Sections 1–8, the data tell a consistent story: paid curb segments spread across the commercial grid (1–2) but are heavily cut by loading, bus, and no-stopping zones (3). Public garages are fewer and more concentrated (4), and where both overlap (5–7), drivers still have multiple options within a short walk (6–8).

Main takeaway: Parking pressure in Seattle doesn’t just come from having too little capacity overall—it also comes from a mismatch between where parking feels available (curb space along the street) and where much of the capacity actually sits (garages tucked just off the main corridors).

12. The Cruising Reality

Often, curb parking isn't just "full" or "empty"—it's a game of chance.

When demand is high (like 95%+ occupancy), finding that one open spot requires circling the block repeatedly. This behavior is called "cruising."

Simulation: Press Start Cruising to simulate a driver looking for parking in a saturated neighborhood. Watch how time ticks up as they check spot after spot.

Summary

Seattle's parking landscape is a complex balance between curb-side convenience and off-street capacity. As shown in this visualization, paid curb spaces dominate the street grid in commercial districts, yet they are frequently restricted by loading zones and no-stopping rules. Public garages provide the bulk of the actual vehicle capacity in dense areas like Downtown, though they are often less visible to the casual driver cruising for a spot.

By understanding the relationship between these two supply types, city planners and drivers alike can make better decisions—whether that means choosing a garage directly to avoid cruising, or adjusting curb regulations to better serve high-demand neighborhoods.
