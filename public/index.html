Seattle’s Parking System: Patterns and Experience

CSE 442 - Data Visualization (Fall 2025)

Exploring Seattle's Curb Space System

Seattle contains thousands of curb spaces and dozens of public garages, yet drivers often experience the system as chaotic and inconsistent. What looks abundant in the data can feel scarce behind the wheel. Much like the MBTA visualization revealed how subway behavior emerges from underlying structure, Seattle’s parking behavior is shaped by the spatial arrangement of curb rules and garage locations. This visualization places both parking worlds—the curb and the garage—onto a single map to uncover patterns that are hard to see at street level.

Curb Spaces Across Seattle

Curb spaces are the first places drivers look, lining nearly every major neighborhood. But they are also the most fragmented part of the system: regulations change block by block, shifting between paid zones, time limits, loading areas, bus lanes, and other restrictions.

By coloring each curb space by district, the map makes these inconsistencies visible. Some neighborhoods have dense, orderly grids of paid parking, while others are dominated by long stretches of restricted or unavailable curb zones. What often feels like “no parking anywhere” is usually the result of this regulatory patchwork.

Public Garages and Lots

Garages form the opposite landscape—large, concentrated, and often hidden from a driver’s natural search pattern. A person circling for curb parking may pass several garages without noticing them, even when they are only a block away.

Plotted alongside the curb spaces, garages reveal a contrasting structure: downtown districts contain tight clusters of garage capacity, while other neighborhoods rely almost entirely on the curb. The map shows that a street with no usable curb spaces may still sit next to ample garage availability—yet drivers rarely treat the two as interchangeable.

Before zooming into a single driver's experience, this section lets you explore how different curb categories and paid parking areas are distributed across Seattle, using the city's open curb-space and garage data.

What This Reveals

Bringing curb spaces and garages together on one map highlights why parking often feels harder than the numbers suggest. Seattle may have plenty of total parking, but the experience is shaped by:

  • Curb spaces that feel convenient but are heavily regulated and unevenly distributed.
  • Garages that are abundant yet visually hidden and easy to miss while circling.
  • District differences that create distinct parking “personalities,” even between adjacent neighborhoods.

From above, it becomes clear that parking scarcity is less about quantity and more about how the system is organized. This visualization shows that the challenge begins long before a driver starts circling—within the structure and layout of Seattle’s parking network.

Seattle’s Parking Network

Seattle’s curbside parking system includes thousands of paid curb spaces and a wide range of public garages and lots. Although these resources are abundant, drivers often experience parking as inconsistent or difficult to find. To understand why, this visualization brings together Seattle’s official curb-space and garage datasets into a unified, interactive map.

The Curb and Garage Layers

Curb segments form the backbone of the system. Each line represents a block face with its own rules—paid areas, categories, time limits, and space lengths. Public garages, on the other hand, are fixed points without neighborhood labels. To compare them meaningfully, each garage is assigned to the nearest paid-parking area or placed in an “Others” category if it lies outside any zone.

Parking Supply by Paid Area

The bar chart above summarizes how curb segments and garages are distributed across Seattle’s paid areas. Grey bars represent curb spaces; blue bars represent garages. Users can toggle each category on or off, and brushing across the bars updates totals based only on the visible parking types. This makes it easy to explore questions such as which areas rely heavily on curb supply versus those supported more by structured parking.

By presenting both layers together, the visualization highlights how the organization of Seattle’s parking network—not just the total amount—shapes the everyday experience of finding a place to park.

Drag across the bars to select a set of paid areas.

The Parking Hunt

Finding parking in downtown Seattle—especially near Pike Place Market—often feels unpredictable. Unlike transit systems with fixed routes, drivers navigate a constantly changing environment shaped by curb regulations, demand, and street layout. What seems straightforward on a map becomes a sequence of small decisions and reversals once the search begins.

Much like the MBTA visualization reveals the daily rhythms of train movement, this animation illustrates the lived experience of parking: not delay, but uncertainty.

What the Animation Shows

The simulation begins with a driver just outside Pike Place Market. Their task is simple: find the nearest available parking space. The car moves realistically across the grid, checking curb spaces and garages one by one. Many look promising but turn out to be full. Others require awkward L-shaped routes because of one-way streets or block geometry.

Each movement updates the “state” of the search, showing how quickly nearby options disappear and how the search radius expands with every failed attempt.

A Typical Parking Search

The visualization highlights what most Seattle drivers already know: the closest spot is rarely the easiest to reach. Several attempts may fail before one succeeds, and a single unavailable space can shift the entire route. This mirrors the MBTA’s observation that even small disruptions can ripple across a system, reshaping behavior far beyond the initial obstacle.

Seeing the Experience From Above

By watching the car wander block by block, viewers can see the structure behind the frustration. Clusters of full spots push the driver outward; isolated available spaces require long detours; garages and curb spaces influence the search differently. From above, the pattern becomes clear: parking difficulty is not random—it is built into the geometry and distribution of the system.

Why This Matters

Understanding the parking experience as a dynamic process, rather than a static map, highlights why parking frustration persists even in cities with abundant total supply. The animation demonstrates that:

  • Difficulty finding parking is systemic, not just anecdotal.
  • Curb regulations create invisible barriers that shape where drivers look.
  • The nearest space is not always the most accessible.
  • Even short trips often involve multiple failed attempts before success.

By connecting the lived experience of the driver with the spatial patterns shown in the first visualization, the Parking Hunt illustrates how the design and distribution of curb and garage spaces influence everyday behavior.
